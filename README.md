# swift-galerkin

Use this package to turn a mesh into the sparse matrices of a finite-element model. Map each element from a reference cell: flat, `Galerkin.Reference.Quadrilateral` (bilinear on [−1, 1]²) or `Galerkin.Reference.Triangle` (linear on (0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1)); solid, `Galerkin.Reference.Hexahedron` (trilinear on [−1, 1]³) or `Galerkin.Reference.Tetrahedron` (linear on (0, 0, 0), (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, 1)). `shape(at:)` gives the shape functions and their derivatives at a point (along ζ too on a solid cell), `Galerkin.Jacobian(_:corners:)` or, for a solid, `Galerkin.Jacobian.Solid(_:corners:)` the map's derivative, its determinant and the derivatives along the axes, and `Galerkin.Jacobian.Surface(_:corners:)` a flat cell's map onto a face in space, such as a shell's or the wall a body of air couples to, with its normal and area element, and `quadrature(degree:)` a Gauss rule exact to that total degree, a `Galerkin.Quadrature<2, Scalar>` or `<3, Scalar>`. Integrate an element's matrix with them; what the matrix means (a plate, a membrane, air) is the caller's. `Galerkin.Unknowns` numbers a fixed count of components at every vertex, skipping held ones; `Galerkin.Rotation` takes two of a vertex's components in a turned frame, which is how a constraint along a curved edge is imposed; `Galerkin.Assembly` adds element matrices, corner by corner, into the entries of a swift-sparse `Sparse.Symmetric`, whose eigenpairs swift-eigen finds.

## Laws

- **Quadrature.** `quadrature(degree: d)` integrates every polynomial of total degree at most `d` exactly, to rounding. The square's and the cube's rules are tensor products of ⌊d/2⌋ + 1 Gauss–Legendre points, so an odd `d` is sharp: one more degree along one axis alone is missed. The triangle's is the square's collapsed onto it (Duffy), with one more point across; the tetrahedron's is the cube's collapsed onto it, with one more point along the second axis and two more along the third. Their points are inside the cell and their weights positive. `Galerkin.Quadrature<1, Scalar>.gauss(count:)` is the one-dimensional rule, nodes ascending.
- **Shape functions.** At every point the shape functions add up to one and their derivatives to zero: they are a partition of unity, so a constant field is reproduced. Each is one at its own corner and zero at the others, and on any element, flat or solid, they reproduce a linear field and its gradient. A flat cell's shape has no ζ derivatives.
- **Jacobian.** The determinant is positive where the element keeps the reference cell's orientation (a flat cell's corners counterclockwise; a solid's in the order of its reference cell, right-handed), and integrated over the reference cell it is the element's area or volume. `cartesian` applies J⁻¹, which also turns covariant components into Cartesian ones.
- **Surface.** `normal` is x,ξ × x,η, square to the face and pointing to where its corners turn counterclockwise; `determinant` is its length, never negative. Integrated over the reference cell it is the face's area, the same for a flat face however it is turned in space, and the area of the warped surface a bilinear face spans.
- **Unknowns.** Numbered from zero, vertex by vertex and component by component, held ones skipped; `count` is the order of the assembled matrices.
- **Rotation.** With t = (cos, sin) and n = (−sin, cos), a vertex's components (a, b) along the axes are p t + q n for its turned components (p, q). An element matrix A becomes Tᵀ A T before it is added, so holding p or q holds the motion along t or n.
- **Assembly.** Element matrices are symmetric, row-major over their corners' components corner by corner. The assembled matrix equals the dense sum of every element's Tᵀ A T, restricted to the unknowns that are not held.
